## Chapter 34 — The Graph

As of the date on the copyright page, the evidence library at
detroit.primals.eco contains 5,858 nodes and 32,894 documented
connections.

What does that mean?

A node is a person, an entity, an institution, or a concept.
Brian Banks is a node. Purpose Charter Academy is a node.
The 14th Amendment is a node. The Walton Family Foundation
is a node. A felony conviction from 1999 is a node.

A connection — an edge, in graph theory — is a documented
relationship between two nodes. Employment. Board membership.
Financial transaction. Family relationship. Court case.
Shared address. Shared attorney. Authorization. Oversight
responsibility. Political endorsement.

Every connection has a type. The graph uses 1,376 distinct
relationship types. Not categories — types. Each type
describes a specific relationship: "audits," "authorized_by,"
"shares_address_with," "endorsed," "failed_to_stop,"
"bankruptcy_filing," "board_member."

Every connection has a source. "Record" means it comes from a
public database — ICHAT, PACER, LARA, FEC, MOECS, the State
Bar. "Structural" means it comes from an observed position
in the network — two entities sharing an address or a board
member. "Null" means the relationship is suspected but
unverified.

The graph is not a conspiracy board. There are no red strings.
There are no hunches. There is data, and there are connections,
and every connection is labeled with its evidence basis.

You can explore the graph at detroit.primals.eco. It is
rendered as a force-directed network — nodes repel each other
like charged particles, edges pull them together like springs.
The resulting shape is not designed. It is emergent. The
clusters that form are not arranged by the author. They are
arranged by the data.

The investigation core — Banks, PCA, Holland, Miller, Moreland,
Dykema, Plante Moran — forms a dense cluster near the center.
Not because we placed them there. Because they are densely
connected to each other. The density IS the evidence.

Brian Banks has 1,077 connections across 155 different
relationship types. He is the most diversely connected node
in the graph. Every direction of inquiry — criminal, financial,
educational, political, familial, legal, regulatory — connects
to him. Not because we targeted him. Because the data connects
to him.

The graph contains 173 articles — analysis documents that ask
specific questions of specific nodes. Each article is itself a
node in the graph, connected to the nodes it discusses.

The graph contains 906 edges of type "asks_directly" — direct
questions aimed at specific individuals and institutions. 476
investigation targets have been asked. Zero have responded.

The graph is open source. The git repository is public. Every
commit is timestamped. Every change is auditable. If we make
an error, the error is visible in the commit history. If we
correct an error, the correction is visible too.

The graph is not finished. It grows. New public records are
added. New connections are documented. New questions are asked.
The investigation is a living system — not a snapshot, but a
process.

If you clone the repository, you own a complete copy. If you
find an error, you can open an issue. If you find a connection
we missed, you can submit it. The proof accepts contributions
from anyone. The proof improves when challenged.

That is the point. The proof is not ours. The proof is the
data. And the data is public.

## Chapter 38 — The Integrity Layer

Every file in the evidence repository is hashed using BLAKE3 —
a modern cryptographic hash function designed for speed and
security.

What does this mean in plain language?

It means that every file has a digital fingerprint. If a single
character in the file is changed — one letter, one number, one
space — the fingerprint changes completely. You cannot modify
the evidence without the modification being detectable.

It means that the evidence has a timestamp. Every git commit
records the exact date and time the change was made, signed
by the author's cryptographic key. You cannot backdate
evidence. You cannot insert evidence "after the fact" without
the insertion being visible in the commit log.

It means that every copy of the repository — all 753 of them —
can be compared against the original. If they match, the
evidence is intact. If they don't, someone modified their copy,
and the modification is provable.

This is not theoretical. This is the same technology that
secures the Bitcoin blockchain, that verifies software updates
for every iPhone, that ensures your bank's website is genuine
and not a phishing site.

Applied to an investigation, it means: the evidence cannot be
tampered with, cannot be backdated, cannot be fabricated after
the fact, and is verified by the same mathematical principles
that secure trillions of dollars in global financial
transactions.

If Brian Banks wants to dispute the evidence, he is welcome
to. The evidence will verify itself. The hashes will match or
they won't. The timestamps will hold or they won't. The
sources will resolve or they won't.

## Chapter 39 — What Is a Zero-Knowledge Proof?
### (For the Person Who Skipped Chapter 13)

Imagine someone gives you a locked box and says: "There is a
red ball inside this box."

You cannot see inside the box. You cannot open it. You don't
trust the person who told you.

But the person says: "Here's a key. Open the box. If the ball
is red, I'm telling the truth. If it's not, I'm lying."

They hand you the key. You open the box. The ball is red.

The person proved their statement was true WITHOUT revealing
how they knew the ball was red, when they put it there, or
why. They didn't reveal their identity. They didn't reveal
their strategy. They revealed one thing: the verifiable truth
of their claim.

That is a zero-knowledge proof.

This book is a zero-knowledge proof.

The claims are in the box. The keys are the public databases
listed in Appendix A. You open each database. You search the
name. The result either matches the claim or it doesn't.

If it matches: the claim is verified. The proof is valid.
If it doesn't: the claim is false, and the author is lying.

You do not need to know who assembled the evidence. You do
not need to trust the author. You do not need to take anyone's
word for anything.

You need ICHAT and a web browser. Fifteen minutes. Nine
convictions will appear. Or they won't.

You need the State Bar database and a web browser. Thirty
seconds. Zero results will appear. Or they won't.

You need PACER and $0.10 per page. Five bankruptcies will
appear. Or they won't.

The proof is not the book. The proof is the databases. The
book just tells you where to look.

In computational complexity theory, P is the class of problems
that can be solved quickly. NP is the class of problems whose
solutions can be VERIFIED quickly. The P versus NP question
asks: are these two classes the same? Can every problem that
is easy to check also be easy to solve?

For this problem: yes. The checking IS the solving. You don't
need to discover the network. You need to read what the
network published about itself.

P equals NP. Here. For this. Because the network WANTS you
to see its credentials and its boards and its grants and its
dinners. It just doesn't want you to check.
