## Chapter 43 — 30 Creatures That Are Real

The world is weirder than fiction. chipoMunk knows this.

**The Platypus** — a mammal that lays eggs, has a bill like a
duck, a tail like a beaver, venom in its hind legs, and
electrolocation (it can detect the electrical fields generated
by the muscles of its prey). When European naturalists first
received a platypus specimen in 1799, they thought it was a
hoax. They tried to find the stitches.

There are no stitches. It's real.

**Tardigrades** — microscopic animals, also called water bears,
that can survive:
- Temperatures from -458°F to 300°F
- Pressures six times greater than the deepest ocean trench
- The vacuum of space (for ten days, on the outside of the ISS)
- Radiation hundreds of times the lethal dose for humans
- Complete dehydration for decades (they curl up into a "tun"
  and wait)

They are nearly indestructible. They are also adorable under
a microscope — they have eight stumpy legs and waddle.

**The Mantis Shrimp** — a marine crustacean that punches its
prey with the force of a .22 caliber bullet. Its strike is so
fast that it creates cavitation bubbles — tiny implosions of
superheated vapor — that deliver a second shockwave after the
initial punch. It sees sixteen types of color receptors (humans
have three). It can perceive ultraviolet light and polarized
light. It is, pound for pound, the most powerful striker in
the animal kingdom.

**The Axolotl** — a salamander that can regenerate its limbs,
heart, spinal cord, and parts of its brain. It is neotenic —
it reaches adulthood without metamorphosing, remaining in its
larval form its entire life. Scientists study it to understand
regeneration. It smiles.

**Slime Mold** (Physarum polycephalum) — not an animal, not a
plant, not a fungus. A single-celled organism that can solve
mazes, replicate the Tokyo rail network (researchers placed
food sources on a map of Tokyo and the slime mold connected
them with a network that matched the rail system), and make
"decisions" without a brain, a nervous system, or any neural
tissue.

A child who learns about the mantis shrimp will never forget
it. A child who learns that slime mold can solve mazes without
a brain will ask: "How?" And that "how" is biology and physics
and mathematics and philosophy — all in one organism.

A child at PCA, in a classroom with an uncertified teacher,
with a worksheet about fractions, with 3% math proficiency,
will never encounter the mantis shrimp unless someone gives
them the encounter.

chipoMunk gives it to them. The graph gives it to them.

The mantis shrimp is in the same graph as Brian Banks. Because
the mantis shrimp is what education looks like when it works:

## Chapter 44 — 51 Nobel Laureates (and One Man with Nine Convictions)
