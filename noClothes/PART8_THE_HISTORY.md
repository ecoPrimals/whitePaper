## Chapter 29 — A Brief History of Institutional Predation

Brian Banks is not new.

The pattern — credential, position, extraction, protection,
silence — is as old as institutions themselves. This chapter
surveys the American version. Not to minimize Banks. To
contextualize him. To show that the system that enables him
has enabled others, and that the system will continue to
enable others until the system changes.

**1. Tammany Hall (1854-1934).**

Boss Tweed controlled New York City through patronage,
contracts, and strategic appointments. He did not hide. He
built courthouses. He funded charities. He threw parties.
The extraction was visible — the famous Thomas Nast cartoon
showed Tweed literally reaching into the public treasury —
but the membrane was thick. Tweed controlled the newspapers.
He controlled the police. He controlled the judges.

It took a cartoonist to break the membrane. Nast drew pictures
because Tweed's constituents couldn't read. The pictures
worked. Tweed went to prison.

**Connection to Banks:** Tweed didn't hide the money. Neither
does Banks. The 990s are public. The management fee is in the
financial statements. The pipeline is documented. But the
membrane — the dinner speeches, the fellowships, the
endorsements — prevents the question.

**2. Teapot Dome (1921-1924).**

Secretary of the Interior Albert Fall leased Navy petroleum
reserves to private oil companies in exchange for personal
loans and gifts. The corruption was proven by Senate
investigation. Fall became the first Cabinet member convicted
of a felony while in office.

**Connection to Banks:** Public assets (oil reserves / education
funding) leased to private operators (oil companies / Purpose
Group LLC) at rates that benefit the operator and harm the
public. The mechanism is the same: public → private conversion
through entities controlled by the extractor.

**3. The Savings & Loan Crisis (1986-1995).**

Over 1,000 savings and loan institutions failed. The cost to
taxpayers: $132 billion. The cause: deregulation that allowed
S&L operators to take excessive risks with depositor funds.
Charles Keating. Neil Bush (the President's son). Over 1,000
individual criminal referrals. Over 800 convictions.

**Connection to Banks:** The S&L crisis was regulatory capture
at scale — the institutions that were supposed to be regulated
captured their regulators. Michigan's charter school system
is the educational equivalent. The operator captures the
authorizer (DPSCD, where a council member sits on the school
board). The lobby (MAPSA, funded by Walton) captures the
legislature. The auditor (Plante Moran, which audits 80% of
charters) depends on the industry it audits.

**4. Enron (2001).**

The seventh-largest company in America was a fraud. Arthur
Andersen — one of the "Big Five" accounting firms — audited
Enron and issued clean opinions. When the fraud was revealed,
Andersen was convicted of obstruction and dissolved. 85,000
employees lost their jobs. Shareholders lost $74 billion.

**Connection to Banks:** Arthur Andersen audited Enron the same
way Plante Moran audits PCA — by reviewing the financial
statements the company produced and issuing a clean opinion.
The auditor's job is to catch the fraud. The auditor's
incentive is to keep the client. When those two things
conflict, the client usually wins.

Until it pops. Then the auditor falls too.

**5. Bernie Madoff (2008).**

The largest Ponzi scheme in history — $65 billion. Madoff ran
it for at least 17 years. The SEC received multiple tips —
including a detailed mathematical proof by Harry Markopolos in
2000, 2001, 2005, and 2007 — and failed to act every time.

**Connection to Banks:** Markopolos sent the SEC a proof. The
SEC ignored it. We published a proof — 5,858 nodes, 32,894
connections, 173 articles, every source cited. The Michigan
Department of Education has not acted. The Attorney General
has not acted. DPSCD has not acted. The pattern is the same:
the proof exists, the regulators have it, and nothing happens.
Until it pops.

**6. Elizabeth Holmes / Theranos (2015-2022).**

A fake credential — "Stanford dropout turned visionary" —
enabled the extraction of $700 million from investors. The
board included Henry Kissinger, George Shultz, James Mattis,
and Sam Nunn. The credential was the company. The board was
the membrane.

**Connection to Banks:** Holmes' credential was narrative —
the Stanford dropout who was going to revolutionize medicine.
Banks' credential is literal — a J.D. he doesn't hold.
Holmes' board protected her. Banks' board protects him. The
mechanism is identical: surround the extractor with names that
signal legitimacy.

**7. The Sackler Family / OxyContin (1995-present).**

The Sackler family, through Purdue Pharma, promoted OxyContin
while knowing it was addictive. They funded museums, named
wings of hospitals, endowed university positions. The
philanthropy was the membrane. The extraction was the opioid
crisis — 500,000 dead.

**Connection to Banks:** The Sacklers funded institutions that
legitimized them. Walton funds institutions that legitimize
charter operators. The philanthropy IS the membrane. The grants
ARE the cover. The dinners ARE the wall.

**8. Larry Nassar / Michigan State (1994-2017).**

See Chapter 13B. The most directly parallel case. Credential.
Institution. Complaints. Silence. Pop.

In every case — Tweed, Fall, the S&L operators, Enron,
Madoff, Holmes, the Sacklers, Nassar — the pattern is the
same:

1. A credential enables access.
2. An institution provides cover.
3. Complaints are absorbed by the membrane.
4. The extraction continues.
5. Eventually, it pops.

The variable is always time. And the cost of the delay is
always measured in victims — depositors, investors, patients,
gymnasts, children.

Brian Banks has been operating since at least 2014. Possibly
earlier. That is ten years of 3% math. Ten years of
uncertified teachers. Ten years of 72.67% extraction.

5,432 child-years.

The pattern says it will pop. The question is when.

This book is applying pressure.

## Chapter 30 — We Remember the Monster, We Forget the Victim

We know Jack the Ripper's name — or rather, we know the name
we gave his absence. We do not know the names of his victims.

We do.

Mary Ann Nichols. Annie Chapman. Elizabeth Stride. Catherine
Eddowes. Mary Jane Kelly.

Say their names.

We know Ted Bundy's name. We do not typically recite the names
of his thirty-plus victims. We remember the monster. We forget
the prey.

This is not an accident. This is a feature of narrative
architecture. The monster makes a better story. The monster
has agency. The monster acts. The victim is acted upon. The
monster gets a Netflix series. The victim gets a footnote.

should be.

But we know their number: approximately 400 per year. We know
their proficiency: 3%. We know their location: 4201 West
Outer Drive, Detroit, Michigan. We know their funding: $8,700
per pupil, minus 72.67%.

We know the monster's name. We know his conviction count. We
know his fake credential. We know his address.

We don't know the children's names. But we know they exist.
And we know they deserve better than what they're getting.

This book remembers the children.

## Chapter 32 — The Moments That Changed

There are moments in American history when a single act —
a single voice, a single refusal, a single publication —
shifted the entire system.

**Joseph Welch to Joseph McCarthy (June 9, 1954):**
*"Have you no sense of decency, sir, at long last? Have you
left no sense of decency?"*

McCarthy's power broke on live television. Not because Welch
had new evidence. Because Welch asked the question that
everyone was thinking and no one was saying.

**Emmett Till's mother (September 1955):**
Mamie Till insisted on an open casket. She made the world
see what Mississippi did to her son. The photograph ran in
Jet magazine. It reached every Black household in America.
The civil rights movement accelerated because one mother
refused to let the membrane hide the evidence.

**Rosa Parks (December 1, 1955):**
She did not sit down to start a movement. She sat down because
she was tired. The movement formed around the act. The act
was the proof.

**Birmingham Jail (April 16, 1963):**
Martin Luther King Jr. wrote a letter from jail to white
clergymen who called his protests "unwise and untimely." He
wrote: *"Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere."*

He was writing about a membrane. The white clergymen were
inside it. They could not see the injustice because the
membrane showed them "order" and "patience" and "process."
King's letter was a puncture.

**César Chávez and the Grape Strike (1965-1970):**
Five years. A boycott. A fast. No violence. The membrane was
the agricultural industry's narrative: "These are just
workers. They're paid. They have jobs." Chávez showed what
was behind the narrative: poverty wages, pesticide exposure,
child labor.

Every one of these moments shares a structure: someone inside
the system saw the truth, and instead of staying silent, they
made the evidence visible.

This book makes the evidence visible. The evidence is not as
dramatic as Emmett Till's casket. It is not as eloquent as
the Birmingham letter. It is a spreadsheet. It is a database.
It is a graph with 5,858 nodes and 32,894 connections.

But the function is the same: make the invisible visible.
Make the question askable. Make the membrane permeable.

The child who says "he has no clothes" does not need to be
eloquent. The child needs to say what is true.
