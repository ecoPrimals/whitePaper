## Chapter 4B — Three Percent

Three percent.

Say it out loud. Three. Percent.

Of the children at Purpose Charter Academy — approximately 400
children, ages five through fourteen — three percent are
proficient in mathematics.

What does that mean in practice?

It means that if you line up a hundred children from PCA
and hand each one a state-standardized math test — a test
designed to measure whether a child can add fractions, read a
graph, or compute the area of a rectangle — ninety-seven of
those children will fail.

Ninety-seven out of a hundred.

Pick any classroom. Twenty-five children. On average, fewer
than one — 0.75, to be exact — will pass. Some classrooms will
have zero. Some will have one child who carries the entire
statistical weight. That child is doing fine. The other
twenty-four are not.

And here is what that number hides: the test is not asking
advanced questions. The M-STEP — Michigan's Student Test of
Educational Progress — measures grade-level proficiency. Not
excellence. Not mastery. Whether you can do what a child your
age should be able to do.

Third graders are asked whether 4 × 3 equals 12.

Seventh graders are asked to solve 2x + 3 = 11.

Ninety-seven percent of PCA students cannot do this.

Compare PCA to its neighbors:

| School | Math Proficiency | Distance from PCA |
|--------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Purpose Charter Academy | **3%** | — |
| Grosse Pointe South High | 52% | 3.2 miles |
| Grosse Pointe North High | 48% | 4.1 miles |
| Detroit average (all public) | 10% | — |

PCA is three times worse than Detroit's already catastrophic
average. A child at PCA has a better chance of being struck by
lightning in their lifetime (1 in 15,300 per year) than of
being proficient in math at their own school (3 in 100 per
year, and falling).

But it gets worse.

Michigan Department of Education publishes a metric called
"Students Making Expected Growth." This measures whether
students who were behind are catching up — not whether they've
arrived, but whether they're moving in the right direction.

At PCA, this number is also near zero.

The children are not catching up. They are falling further
behind. Every year they spend at PCA is a year they don't get
back. A year of fractions they didn't learn. A year of
multiplication they can't do. A year of reading comprehension
that didn't arrive. A year closer to high school, then
adulthood, then a world that will measure them by their ability
to do the things their school never taught them.

Three percent is not a failing grade. Three percent is an
indictment.

And the man who runs the school takes home 72.67% of its
revenue through a single-member LLC he controls.

$4.9 million a year. Three percent math.

## Chapter 4C — The Two Out of Fourteen

Three percent math proficiency is a symptom. What's the disease?

Start with the teachers.

Michigan maintains a database called MOECS — the Michigan
Online Educator Certification System. It is public. You can
search any teacher's name and see whether they hold a valid
Michigan teaching certificate, what subjects they are
certified to teach, and whether their certification is current.

We searched every instructional staff member listed on PCA's
transparency page.

Of fourteen instructional staff: **two** hold valid Michigan
teaching certificates.

Two out of fourteen. Fourteen percent.

The other twelve are teaching without state certification. They
may hold bachelor's degrees. They may hold certifications from
other states. They may hold nothing at all. What they do not
hold is the credential that the State of Michigan requires to
teach children in a public school.

Michigan law is clear. MCL 380.1531 requires that "a teacher
employed to provide instruction in a public school shall hold
a valid teaching certificate." The law does not say "should."
It says "shall." It is mandatory.

Purpose Charter Academy is a public school — it receives public
funds through the State School Aid Fund. Its students take
state-standardized tests. Its authorizer (DPSCD) is a public
entity. It is subject to the same credentialing requirements
as every other public school in Michigan.

Twelve of its fourteen teachers do not meet those requirements.

Now do the math.

If your school has fourteen teachers and only two are certified,
what is the probability that your child's teacher — the person
standing in front of them five days a week, eight hours a day,
for an entire school year — is actually qualified to be there?

Two out of fourteen. 14.3%.

Your child has an 85.7% chance of being taught by someone who
has not demonstrated competency in the subject they teach, has
not completed a supervised student teaching program, and has
not been evaluated by the State of Michigan.

This is not a staffing problem. Staffing problems look like
thirteen out of fourteen. Twelve and you're getting there.
Two out of fourteen is a policy. It is a decision. It costs
less to hire uncertified staff. The salary savings flow to the
operator — through the Purpose Group LLC, through the single-
member company, to Brian Banks.

The children get three percent.
