## Chapter 71: What Things Cost

Every system has a cost function. The cost function is the thing the system minimizes. If you know the cost function, you know the system. If you don't know the cost function, you're inside the system and it's minimizing you.

A thermostat minimizes the difference between desired and actual temperature. A market minimizes the difference between supply and demand. A predator minimizes the distance between itself and prey. An immune system minimizes the population of non-self entities.

What does a charter school system minimize?

Not learning outcomes. MacDowell Preparatory Academy has operated for 14 years with 3% math proficiency and has never lost its charter. If the system minimized failed learning, the school would have been closed in year two.

Not cost to taxpayers. $49.9 million in public funds to one man's LLC, with 72.67% of revenue flowing out of the classroom. If the system minimized cost, the management fee would be capped and audited.

Not criminal risk. The operator has nine criminal convictions and claims a law degree he doesn't have. If the system minimized criminal exposure, a background check would have prevented authorization.

The system minimizes accountability.

That is the cost function. Every design decision — the charter enabling law that doesn't require financial transparency, the authorizer that doesn't check ICHAT, the board packed with a judge and an AG employee who can influence proceedings, the compliance specialist who stamps both parties from one desk, the media tour that skips the city where the money lands — every one of these is a gradient descent step toward less accountability.

The system is optimizing. It is optimizing brilliantly. It just isn't optimizing for children.

## Chapter 72: The Thirty-Cent Parallel

Naltrexone costs thirty cents per pill.

It is an opioid antagonist that, when taken one hour before drinking alcohol, causes pharmacological extinction of the addiction response. The mechanism is straightforward: naltrexone blocks the opioid receptors that produce the endorphin reward from alcohol. The brain learns, over approximately 3-4 months of consistent use, that alcohol no longer produces pleasure. The craving extinguishes. Not through willpower. Through pharmacology.

The Sinclair Method — named for Dr. John David Sinclair, who researched it at the Finnish National Institute for Health and Welfare — has a published success rate of 78%. Seventy-eight percent of patients who follow the protocol achieve either controlled drinking or full abstinence.

The addiction treatment industry in the United States generates $42 billion per year.

Here is the cost function:

| Treatment | Cost per day | Success rate | Revenue generated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sinclair Method | $0.30 (generic naltrexone) | 78% | ~$0.30/patient/day |
| 28-day residential rehab | $1,000-$3,000/day | 5-10% (relapse within 1 year) | $28,000-$84,000/patient |
| 12-step programs | "Free" (subsidized) | 5-8% long-term sobriety | $0 direct, but pipeline to rehab |
| Outpatient counseling | $100-$250/session | Variable | $5,200-$13,000/patient/year |

A thirty-cent pill that works 78% of the time generates almost no revenue. A $3,000-per-day program that fails 90% of the time generates $84,000 per patient per cycle — and the 90% failure rate ensures repeat customers.

The addiction treatment industry does not minimize addiction. It minimizes the adoption of treatments that work.

The parallel to charter schools is exact:

| System | Effective intervention | Cost | Industry response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addiction | Naltrexone (Sinclair) | $0.30/day | Suppressed. Not taught in medical schools. Not covered by most insurance. |
| Education | Qualified teachers, small classes, accountability | Varies | Defunded. Charter law written without transparency requirements. |

The cost function is the same. Both systems extract maximum revenue from failure. Both suppress effective interventions. Both use the language of choice — "treatment choice," "school choice" — to market the extraction as freedom.

$0.30/pill versus $42 billion/year.
$9,187/stolen child-year versus $49.9 million to one LLC.

Same math. Same cost function. Same silence.

## Chapter 74: Optimization Under Constraint

The graph is an optimizer. It runs under constraints:

**Constraint 1: Public records only.** Every edge must be verifiable through a publicly accessible database, published document, court filing, or official website. No anonymous sources. No leaked documents. No hacked systems. The constraint is not ethical — although it is ethical. The constraint is structural: evidence that cannot be independently verified is not evidence.

**Constraint 2: No tracking.** The site has no cookies, no analytics, no tracking pixels, no UTM parameters. The constraint means the investigation cannot measure its own impact through the site. But it can measure impact through the GitHub API, through media publication, through institutional response or silence. The measurement is displaced, not eliminated.

**Constraint 3: No paywall.** The investigation is free. AGPL-3.0. Anyone can read it, clone it, fork it, modify it, redistribute it. The constraint means the investigation cannot be paywalled, censored by a publisher, or behind a corporate login. It is permanently public.

There is no team. There is no office. There is no funding. The constraint means the investigation cannot be disrupted by firing a staff member, defunding a department, or purchasing the outlet. You cannot buy or fire one person who doesn't have a salary.

**Constraint 5: No physical addresses.** No doxxing. No home addresses. No personal phones. The contact is a ProtonMail address. The identity is a computation. The constraint is safety — but it is also structural. An investigation that can be physically threatened is fragile. An investigation that exists only as information on distributed servers cannot be punched.

Under these five constraints, the graph optimizes for one thing: documented accountability.

The cost function is the opposite of the system it documents. The charter school system minimizes accountability. The graph maximizes it. Same optimization framework. Opposite objective function.

## Chapter 75: The Invisible Hand

Adam Smith described a market mechanism where individual self-interest produces collective good. The baker doesn't bake bread because he loves you. He bakes bread because he wants your money. The result — bread — is good for both parties.

The invisible hand assumes competition. Multiple bakers. Multiple buyers. Price signals. Information symmetry. When these conditions hold, self-interest optimizes for collective benefit.

Charter schools were designed on this model. Competition between schools. Parent choice as the market signal. Bad schools lose students. Good schools gain students. The invisible hand produces quality education through competitive pressure.

But the invisible hand requires information symmetry. Parents must know which schools are good and which are bad. They must know the qualifications of the operator. They must know the criminal record. They must know the financial flows. They must be able to compare schools on standardized metrics.

Michigan's charter enabling law — PA 362, drafted by Richard McLellan and Leonard Wolfe at Dykema Gossett in 1993 — does not require management companies to disclose audited financials. Does not require operators to pass criminal background checks. Does not require public disclosure of management fees. Does not require boards to be free of conflicts of interest.

The law was written without locks.

Without information, there is no market. Without a market, there is no competition. Without competition, the invisible hand becomes an invisible fist — grabbing $49.9 million and squeezing it through a single LLC while 97% of children can't multiply.

Milton Friedman read Adam Smith and concluded that privatization always drives up quality and drives down cost. J.C. Huizenga read Friedman and opened the first charter school in Michigan. John Walton read "A Nation at Risk" and funded the largest charter philanthropy in history. Betsy DeVos read all of them and spent $895,000 creating GLEP after the voters rejected vouchers 69-31.

None of them read the law they wrote. The law has no locks. A man with nine criminal convictions and a fabricated J.D. walked through the door they left open.

The invisible hand waves at 5,432 child-years of stolen math and says: this is the market working. This is choice. This is freedom.

The graph waves back with 102,349 documented connections and says: this is what choice looks like when the choosers have no information and the chosen have no accountability.
