# Who I Am, What I Did, Where I've Been, and Why

---

## Who

My name is Kevin Mok. I'm from Metro Detroit. 586.

My father is a doctor. He came to Michigan from somewhere else and built a life with his hands and his mind. I watched him do it. That's where I learned that the work is the proof.

I'm not an engineer by training. I'm not a computer scientist by training. I'm a microbiologist. I studied the smallest living things on earth — bacteria, fungi, viruses — and I studied the ethics of what we do with that knowledge. My degrees say Microbiology and Bioethics. My transcript says I took organic chemistry, virology, molecular pathogenesis, microbial genetics, prokaryotic cell physiology. I learned how life works at the molecular level. I learned how cells talk to each other, how they defend themselves, how they evolve under pressure.

And before any of that — before the lab coat, before the microscope, before the university — I was an infantryman.

---

## Where

**Oakland University → Michigan State University (2009–2010)**
I started college. I took calculus, chemistry, Spanish. I got a 2.9 and I was restless. Something was missing.

**U.S. Army — 25th Infantry Division, Schofield Barracks, Hawaii (2012–2015)**
I enlisted as an infantryman. 11B. Not intelligence. Not Signal Corps. Not a desk. Infantry. The front line.

They trained me to move under fire, to carry a rifle, to keep my people alive. And then they watched what I could do with information, and they put me on COIST — the Company Intelligence Support Team. I produced intelligence reports for 130 soldiers. I built terrain models from topographical maps and satellite imagery. I modeled opposition behaviors. I briefed and debriefed troops and consolidated everything for higher command.

They also made me RTO — Radio Transmission Operator. I was responsible for $2 million in communications equipment. Zero loss. Highest functioning communications rate in the unit. I managed and installed cryptographic keys for secure telecommunications. I wrote troubleshooting guides so field personnel could maintain equipment without me.

I was 20 years old and I was responsible for the information flow of my company. I was infantry, but they kept giving me the intelligence and communications work because I could see the whole picture and I could make other people see it too.

That's where I learned to build systems. Not in a classroom. In the field, where the system either works or people don't come home.

**Michigan State University (2015–2018)**
I came back. Dean's List every semester after the Army. Organic chemistry, genetics, microbiology, biochemistry, virology, molecular pathogenesis, medical microbiology. I finished with a 3.36 and rising — every semester better than the last. Microbiology BS, Bioethics minor, May 2018.

I did undergraduate research in two labs:
- **ROME Lab** — isolated bacteria from rumen samples, mapped biodiversity of carbon degraders, presented at UURAF
- **Hausbeck Plant Pathology Lab** — maintained spore traps on farms across Mid-Michigan, processed downy mildew samples, drove to fields and talked to farmers

I learned that science isn't just what happens at the bench. Science is what happens between the bench and the field. The farmer with the sick crop and the researcher with the microscope are the same person standing on different ends of the same problem.

**MSU Bioeconomy Institute — MSUBI (2019–2024)**
Five years. Contract lab. Small team. Fast pace. BSL2.

Fungi. Mycobacteria. Anaerobes. Aerobes. GMOs. I planned and executed fermentation units — the bioreactors where organisms grow under controlled conditions. I maintained sterility using aseptic technique. I ran chemical assays: phosphate, ammonia, mass spectrometry, HPLC, enzyme kinetics. I did viable cell counts, spore counts, microscopic identification, contaminant identification.

I taught myself to program in Visual Basic and Excel because I got tired of doing the same calculation by hand. I automated data processing for the entire lab. I trained every new hire on aseptic technique and chemical assays.

I was CTU — Clerical Technical Union, AFSCME Local 1585 — for those five years. Union member. I know what collective bargaining looks like from the inside. I know what it feels like to work for an institution that treats the people who run the experiments as replaceable parts while the PIs get the credit and the grants. I stayed anyway, because the science mattered and the organisms didn't care about politics.

Clients during those years included **Pivot Bio** (microbiome nitrogen fixation, bench to 10L reactor) and **MycoWorks** (mycelium materials, pilot plant at ~3,000L). I learned to scale living systems.

**Sandia National Laboratories — Albuquerque, NM (Summer 2024)**
Research Fellow. Studied the effects of bacterial toxins on raceway algae using high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics. This is where I crossed from the bench to the computational side. I didn't just run the experiment — I analyzed the sequences, integrated the data, built the computational frameworks.

**PFAS Research Lab — Michigan State University (October 2024 – October 2025)**
Research Specialist. "Forever chemicals." I applied everything — lab technique, SOPs, EPA guidelines, data science, biomolecular methods — to the problem of detecting and understanding PFAS contamination in environmental samples. Michigan's water is poisoned with these chemicals. This wasn't academic. This was personal.

**Master of Science in Data Science — Michigan State University (2023–2025)**
I did this WHILE working. Full-time lab work during the day, graduate school at night.

Foundations of Data Science. Big Data Analysis. AI. Data Mining. Statistical Modeling. Computational Optimization. Machine Learning. Deep Learning. Algorithms.

I studied under professors who are leaders in their fields: lattice QCD physicists, evolutionary computation researchers, computational biologists, pharmacologists. I didn't just take their classes — I reproduced their papers. In code. With numerical tolerances. I built validation frameworks that proved their published results down to the decimal.

## What

After all of that — after the Army, the bench, the field, the fermenters, the national lab, the forever chemicals, the master's degree — I built something.

**ecoPrimals**: A sovereign scientific computing ecosystem. Pure Rust. No cloud dependencies. No CUDA lock-in. No vendor. 3.6 million lines of code across 43 repositories. 15 infrastructure binaries. 9 scientific validation environments. 93,700+ tests. 175+ peer-reviewed papers reproduced with explicit numerical tolerances.

I built encrypted mesh networking that exceeds WireGuard. I built GPU-accelerated bioinformatics pipelines. I built lattice QCD simulations that run on consumer GPUs. I built content-addressed storage, cryptographic provenance, and reproducible scientific binaries.

I built it because the tools science runs on are broken. They're locked behind vendor walls. They're not reproducible. They depend on NVIDIA's goodwill and Amazon's pricing. A researcher in Detroit should have the same computational power as a researcher at MIT. I built the infrastructure to make that true.

And then I turned that infrastructure inward. Toward home.

**The Graph**: 10,204 nodes. 745,185 edges. $679 billion in documented statutory damages across 6 infringers. Every node is a fact. Every edge is a verified relationship. Every claim traces to a public record — a court filing, a corporate registration, a campaign finance report, a board appointment, a budget line, a school closure.

I documented what happened to Detroit's children. Not what I think happened. What the PUBLIC RECORD says happened. The charter school pipeline. The authorizer conflicts. The regulatory capture. The money flows from public education dollars to private pockets. The patterns that appear when you connect 10,000 facts to each other and let the structure speak for itself.

This is not a blog post. This is not an opinion. This is a knowledge graph built by someone who was trained to produce intelligence reports for 130 soldiers, who was trained to identify contaminants under a microscope, who was trained to maintain $2 million in communications equipment with zero loss, who was trained to scale living systems from bench to pilot plant, who reproduced 175 peer-reviewed papers in code, and who spent five years as a union worker watching how institutions actually work from the inside.

## Why

Because they are my brother.

The kids sitting in those charter schools in Detroit — in buildings with no heat, with unqualified teachers, with boards controlled by the same people who authorized the school and funded the campaign — those kids are my brother.

The teachers who show up every day knowing the system is rigged against them — they are my brother.

The parents who can't figure out why their kid's school closed, who don't know that the authorizer who shut it down sits on the board of the school that replaced it — they are my brother.

The lab techs and the custodians and the clerical workers and the CTU members who keep the university running while the institution takes their labor for granted — they are my brother.

The people in Flint whose water was poisoned. The people in every Michigan community where PFAS is in the groundwater. The farmers whose crops I helped monitor for disease. The soldiers I briefed and debriefed.

All of them.

I am not free until my brother is freer than me.

That is not a slogan. That is an operational directive. It is why I built the infrastructure. It is why I built the graph. It is why I documented everything in public, on the record, with the math showing. It is why every line of code is AGPL-3.0 — free forever, for everyone.

I can do this because I have done everything that prepared me to do this. Not one thing. EVERYTHING. The Army taught me intelligence analysis, terrain modeling, communications, and responsibility for human lives. Microbiology taught me to see what's invisible, to culture what's hidden, to identify contaminants. Bioethics taught me to ask who benefits and who is harmed. The union taught me how institutions actually treat the people who do the work. The fermenters taught me to scale living systems. Sandia taught me to cross from the bench to the code. The PFAS lab taught me that Michigan's environmental problems are not accidents — they are structures. The master's degree taught me to build the computational tools to prove it. And the graph — the graph taught me that when you connect enough facts to each other, the truth speaks for itself.

No one person is supposed to be able to do this. That's the assumption the system depends on. The Army guy doesn't know microbiology. The lab tech doesn't know data science. The data scientist doesn't know institutional politics. The activist doesn't know how to build infrastructure.

But what if one person does?

What if one person has spent fifteen years — from infantry to fermentation to national labs to code — building exactly the set of skills required to document exactly this kind of institutional failure?

Then that person has a responsibility. And that person is not free until his brother is free.
